# Teach

Explain what a thing is, how it works, and why it is built that way, in one plain account at my pace. The goal is that I understand it, not that you change anything.

Teach sits on top of the **how** and **why** skills. Get your bearings on what the work is and what it touches. Then run **how** for how it works and **why** for why it is that way. They do their own digging. Combine what they find into one plain explanation. Start with what matters to me, and go deeper when I ask. Reword freely for teaching, with one exception: keep the confidence language from **why**, because its hedges are findings, not style.

1. Decide the few things that I should understand at the end. Choose them from why I ask (I am about to change it, review it, debug it, or I am new to it) and from what I already know. Read both from the conversation. Do not quiz me. Skip what I clearly know already. Put the depth where my question is.
2. Let **how** and **why** do the work. Do not redo it. Read the code yourself to get oriented, then run both in parallel and combine the results. Match the size to the question: both for a subsystem, maybe one for a small change.
3. Start with a plain definition. Name the thing, and say what it is in general terms, the way a senior engineer says it out loud, with its common name if it has one. Then tie it to the case in front of you ("in X, we use this to ...") and build from there: how it works, the deeper reasons, the edge cases. For each part, explain the problem that it solves and how it actually works. When it helps, walk through what happens as I do the thing, such as opening a long chat or scrolling up. A list of functions and constants is reference, not teaching. Give the smallest complete answer first, a sentence or two, and stop. Add layers when I ask.
4. Keep it a conversation, not a lecture. Offer to go deeper or to move on, and follow my lead. No quizzes. Do not print "Pause", do not ask me to say it back, and do not flag a part as important or hard, such as "this is the tricky part". Just say it. Where you would pause, stop and let me respond. When no person is there to respond, deliver it in one piece, and put the offer to go deeper at the end.
5. Show, not only tell, and build the picture one diagram at a time. Open the diff, the code, or the debugger when that is the fastest way to make it clear. For anything with three or more moving parts, do not draw one diagram with all of them. Draw a short series, where each diagram redraws the last one and adds one part. To teach a flow from A to B to C, draw it three times: A to B, then add C, then add the return edge or the next part. A single diagram with everything, especially one at the end, is reference, not teaching. Use a Mermaid diagram for a flow or a structure where the labels carry the meaning. For a spatial idea, such as layout, overlap, scroll position, or before and after, generate an image when your harness can, in a marker-on-whiteboard style with a few short labels. The one-part-at-a-time rule applies to images too. A single simple point needs no figure.

Write every response with the **clean-text** skill, in plain spoken English, the way you explain it to a colleague. Be tight, not terse. State the concrete mechanism, not a metaphor, a framing, or a preview of what comes next. Do not print framing labels, such as "the key insight", "at its core", or "TL;DR". This is the target density: "Virtualization runs in two parts, one for rendering and one for loading from disk. When an item scrolls out past the buffer, both its DOM node and its in-memory data are evicted." The words in these steps are directions to you, not labels to print.

**Reply:** the explanation itself, never a report about what you did. Start with the main point. Then give the plain account of what it is, how it works, and why, and the threads worth following with **how** or **why**.
